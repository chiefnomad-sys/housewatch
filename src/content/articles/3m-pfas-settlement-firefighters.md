3M has agreed to pay $7.35 billion to settle personal injury claims from people who say its PFAS chemicals made them sick. A large share of them are firefighters who sprayed or trained with the company's firefighting foam.

The company announced the deal on November 3. It covers about 115,000 to 120,000 eligible plaintiffs, according to 3M, which says that's roughly 99% of the personal injury claims it faces.

That's a lot of money. It's also spread thin and paid slowly. Here's what's in it for the people on the job.

## Six conditions qualify

The settlement agreement, which 3M filed with federal regulators, lists six injuries that count:

- Kidney cancer
- Testicular cancer
- Liver cancer
- Thyroid cancer
- Ulcerative colitis
- Thyroid disease

That's the whole list. Other cancers firefighters get at high rates, like bladder, prostate and colon cancer, aren't on it.

The agreement covers people exposed through drinking water and people exposed "through a means other than" drinking water, which takes in foam on the fireground and in training. The Fire Law Blog, which follows fire service legal cases, reported that it can also include exposure tied to turnout gear. Gear makers themselves aren't part of this deal, and claims against them go on.

## The money comes slowly

3M puts the deal's present-day value at about $5.8 billion, since most of it is paid years from now.

- **2026:** an initial $50 million.
- **2027:** nothing.
- **2028 to 2033:** six payments of about $1.045 billion each, according to the agreement.
- **Reserve:** up to $680 million is set aside for future claims.

Individual payouts will be set by a points system in an attachment to the agreement that hasn't been made public. So nobody can tell you yet what a kidney cancer claim is worth.

The deal only goes forward if enough eligible plaintiffs sign on. If too few do, 3M and the plaintiffs' side can walk away.

## Read the release before you sign

To get paid, you give up your PFAS claims against 3M. The Fire Law Blog reports that includes claims for illnesses beyond the six on the list. If you're sick with something that isn't covered, or worried you might be later, that matters.

3M doesn't admit it did anything wrong. In its announcement, CEO Bill Brown called the deal a significant milestone that "substantially reduces litigation uncertainty" for the company.

Fred Thompson, an attorney at Motley Rice, which represents plaintiffs, told MPR News the deal is welcome news for people who have spent nearly a decade looking for answers and need help with medical bills.

## What to do now

- **If you already have a lawyer on a foam case:** expect to hear from them about whether you're eligible and whether to opt in. Ask what you'd be releasing.
- **If you don't have a lawyer but have one of the six conditions:** the agreement covers some claims that were never filed in court. Whether you can still get in depends on dates the public documents don't spell out. Talk to a lawyer who handles these cases soon.
- **If you're healthy:** keep records of where and when you used foam, including training. They'll matter for any future claim.

The case sits in federal court in South Carolina, where Judge Richard Gergel oversees thousands of foam lawsuits. The settlement doesn't end claims against other chemical makers or the companies that make turnout gear.
