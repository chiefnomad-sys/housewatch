Sunday night's fire in Rochester, N.Y., went to a fifth alarm, and that took every line company the department has. All of them went to one vacant building: 1447 St. Paul St., a six-story structure in the former Kodak Hawkeye plant on the east side of the Genesee River.

The call came in about 10:15 p.m. on Sunday, Nov. 22, the Democrat and Chronicle reported. More than 50 firefighters responded, WHEC reported. Crews had the fire under control shortly after midnight and stayed into Monday morning on overhaul. One firefighter went to the hospital with injuries that were not life-threatening, and WROC reported the firefighter is expected to be OK.

It is the kind of building every city has and every crew knows: vacant, dark, packed with someone else's junk, with people inside who are not supposed to be there, and big enough that getting water to the fire is its own job.

## What crews found

Crews arrived to heavy smoke and flames, WHEC reported, and 13WHAM reported that firefighters saw people leaving the building. Most of the fire was on the third floor, where cardboard boxes and other stored materials fed it. In a news release quoted by the Democrat and Chronicle, Lt. Howard Villegas of the Rochester Fire Department said the volume of material, smoke and heat made pockets of fire "particularly challenging" to find and put out.

The building had no power. Crews stretched multiple hose lines while others ventilated to slow the spread, according to WHEC. Drones with infrared cameras worked overhead to size up the fire and find hot spots. Inside, darkness and debris slowed everything down.

Villegas described the approach to WROC: "before we enter the building, we try to open it up, get as much visibility as we can." Past that, Villegas told WROC, crawling over boxes and stored items with very little visibility can get dangerous.

## Water was the problem

"Honestly, the biggest issue was water supply," Villegas told WHEC.

Villegas told WROC the trouble was scale: in a structure that large, getting enough hose, pressure and water to fire far inside the building was a challenge on its own. The Hawkeye complex covers about 759,000 square feet across seven buildings on 12 acres, the Democrat and Chronicle reported.

## Who was inside

The building is supposed to be empty. Villegas told WHEC that a group of people camping inside got out safely. Investigators have spoken with at least one person who was inside, WHEC reported. The cause is under investigation.

Sunday was not the first trouble there. City property records show six police and fire incidents at the building over three years, WHEC reported, and Rochester police say they have made 11 arrests for burglary and trespassing since May. The city sealed the building last year, but a WHEC reporter found several ways in, including a hole in the fence and a door. On Monday, a second fire broke out while that reporter was back at the building, and a woman who appeared to be living inside ran out as firefighters arrived.

The plant closed in 2011. In July, the site was classified as a Class 2 Superfund site, WHEC and 13WHAM reported, and WROC reported that earlier this year the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation declared the site a public safety hazard after elevated trichloroethylene and dichloroethane turned up in the groundwater.

Grace Ren, who lives in Illinois, bought the building in April, according to WHEC. WROC lists Linked Home Investment LLC as the current owner. Ren told WHEC that the building's problems came to light only after the purchase. Villegas told WROC that paying for 24-hour security on an empty building is beyond a lot of owners.

## What comes next

The mayor's office is working with Ren to get the building sealed and secured, and the city's law department contacted Ren after Monday's fires, WHEC reported. The fire department's cause and origin investigation is still open. For Rochester crews, the open question is whether the ways in get closed before the next run to St. Paul Street.
