Since June 1, a firefighter on the payroll of a Texas city or other local government is owed a free, confidential screening every year starting in their fifth year on the job. The state's Wade Cannon Act, House Bill 198, lists what goes in it: cancer testing, a breast cancer screening, blood and urine tests, a pulmonary function test, an electrocardiogram, an infectious disease screening and a chest X-ray once every five years. Departments that already run an annual medical exam that substantially follows National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) standards can file that plan with the Texas Commission on Fire Protection instead.

The law covers employees. It defines a firefighter by pointing to the state Government Code's definition of fire protection personnel, Section 419.021, which is written around permanent, full-time employees. Most of the people who show up to fires in Texas are not employees.

About two-thirds of the state's firefighting workforce are volunteers, including most of rural Texas, according to Samikshya Badal, lead author of a study from the USA Center for Rural Public Health Preparedness at the Texas A&M University School of Public Health. The center's Nov. 12 release on the study notes that HB 198 applies only to career firefighters.

Most of those volunteers go without a yearly screening. Of 326 Texas volunteers surveyed, 86, or 26.4%, said they get an annual occupational health screening.

## What the survey asked

The team ran an online survey in English and Spanish in August and September 2024. Volunteers answered 29 questions about whether they get screened and what gets in the way. The results were published Sept. 29 in the journal Workplace Health & Safety.

Respondents could name more than one barrier. The ones that came up most:

- **Cost:** 73.4%
- **Not knowing where to get screened:** 39.3%
- **No health insurance:** 38.4%
- **Their doctor doesn't offer it:** 36.5%
- **No time:** 36.2%

Stigma came in at 12.3%, and 10.1% said they didn't want a screening, both far below the access problems at the top of the list.

Insurance is a barrier even for volunteers who have it. Only 11% of the respondents said they were uninsured, yet 38.4% named lack of health insurance as a barrier, so many insured volunteers still counted it against getting screened.

Most respondents fit volunteer service around other work: 73.6% hold full-time jobs, and 44.8% serve rural areas. In the authors' analysis, full-time employment, a rural service area, older age and more education were each tied to reporting certain barriers.

## Why the screening matters

Volunteers face occupational exposures linked to heart disease, lung and breathing illness, mental health problems and cancer, according to the study's background section. NFPA recommends regular occupational health screening, but the authors note that the standard is voluntary for departments to adopt and does not spell out a detailed protocol. That leaves each department to work out what a screening should include.

"We know that early detection leads to early treatment and an overall better quality of life," Badal said in the Texas A&M release.

## What the researchers want

The authors recommend three fixes. The first is bringing screenings to volunteers through partnerships between volunteer departments and health organizations, telehealth and mobile medical clinics, with patient navigators to walk people through the process. The second is health education aimed at firefighters and their families about the risks and the value of regular screening. The third is tightening NFPA's voluntary screening standards into detailed protocols, since the current inconsistency costs volunteers time and money.

None of that is in law. HB 198 was written for career departments, and the study's numbers come from one online survey of 326 volunteers in one state, so they may not hold for every department.

For a volunteer in Texas, the law that took effect June 1 does not cover you, so it requires no one to offer you a screening. If you go without a yearly screening, you are in the majority: about three in four volunteers surveyed do, and cost is the reason most often given. The fix the researchers are pushing for, partnerships with health organizations and mobile clinics that bring the screening to the station, would come from volunteer departments and health organizations working together. Nothing in state law requires it.
