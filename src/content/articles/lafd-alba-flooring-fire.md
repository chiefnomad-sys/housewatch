The knockdown came at 5:24 a.m. on Monday, Nov. 9, 4 hours and 22 minutes after the 911 call. The fire wasn't finished. Los Angeles Fire Department crews stayed on the property for five more days, and it took heavy equipment and a remote-controlled robot to get to the end of it.

The call came in at about 1:02 a.m. for a fire near South Alameda Street and East 55th Street, in the Central-Alameda neighborhood near Vernon. First-arriving LAFD units found a 100-by-150-foot commercial building fully involved, according to the department's incident post. That building is one of four connected structures housing an industrial flooring business that deals in hardwood and cork. The business hasn't been named publicly.

## Offensive, then mostly not

Crews started offensive. LAFD said the volume and intensity of fire overwhelmed the structure, and Incident Command upgraded to a Major Emergency and switched to a defensive strategy. About 200 LAFD firefighters worked the fire, with help from the Los Angeles County Fire Department.

Anyone who has worked a connected commercial complex will catch this: the whole fireground didn't go defensive. While master streams from aerial ladder pipes, along with large-diameter handlines, held the main body of fire, firefighters kept an offensive attack going inside the adjoining building, a two-story metal-clad structure the fire had already extended into. That's the place to stop it before it runs the rest of the complex. Photos LAFD posted show the walls of the main building buckled and warped from the heat.

There were other things on the property to deal with. High-tension lines were arcing, and LADWP shut them down. FOX 11 reported that crews also found propane tanks on site and kept them safe.

## Knocked down isn't out

LAFD's release said overhaul meant chasing "pockets of fire hidden within large quantities of palletized storage," as quoted by the Los Angeles Times. Stacked flooring stock behind buckled walls is not something you pull apart by hand with a pike pole.

So the department brought in its Heavy Equipment units and RS3 to expose the hidden fire and put it out. Companies held small pockets of fire while the heavy equipment crews worked, LAFD said. Around 4 a.m. on Tuesday, Nov. 10, the structure flared up again, and several more companies were attached to what was now the Alba Incident. Flames damaged several power poles, and LADWP crews made repairs on scene.

RS3 is not new. LAFD put it in service in October 2020 and called it the first robotic firefighting vehicle in the United States. Built by Textron's Howe & Howe Technologies, it weighs 3,500 pounds, flows 2,500 gallons per minute, carries a plow blade for pushing debris and is run by remote with high-definition video feedback. LAFD assigned it to Fire Station 3 downtown, with the Urban Search and Rescue task force. When LAFD introduced it, large commercial fires were among the jobs it was meant for. A flooring building with buckled walls and stacked pallets is close to that job description.

## Six days on the property

The smoke stuck around. On Nov. 10, the South Coast Air Quality Management District issued a particle pollution advisory, and LAFD warned that ash could fall on the neighborhood. On Nov. 11, LAFD spokesman Nick Prange said the next priorities were cutting smoke output and making the area safe enough for LADWP workers to repair lines and restore power.

By Nov. 13, Battalion Chief John Patterson reported that neighborhood power was fully restored and that crews found no new hot spots overnight in the exterior debris field. Crews were working toward final extinguishment inside the building, after heavy equipment spent Nov. 12 digging out deep-seated hot spots.

At 10 a.m. on Nov. 14, LAFD declared operations complete, handed the site back to the property owner and sent the field incident management team and citywide crews home. The department's wrap-up summed up the job: defensive operations, cooling smoldering debris, continuous air monitoring and heavy equipment to dismantle the building.

No injuries to civilians or firefighters were reported. The cause has not been confirmed.

## The part crews will recognize

The four-hour knockdown was the short part of this job. Six days is a long time to keep companies, heavy equipment and utility crews on the same lot. LAFD's own timeline is a decent reference for anyone whose first-due includes a district of connected warehouses with stacked stock. Build the flare-up, the heavy equipment and a week of power company work into the plan, not the after-action.
